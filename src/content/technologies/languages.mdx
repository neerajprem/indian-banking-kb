## Why it matters

A bank's software outlives most of the people who write it. A language chosen today will need to be read, patched and extended in ten or twenty years. The right choice balances what the language is good at with how many people can work in it, how stable its ecosystem is and how well its tools support security and correctness.

## Choosing a language for a new system

Four questions settle most choices:
- **What does the system do?** Transaction processing favours Java or Kotlin; infrastructure favours Go; data and models favour Python; interfaces favour TypeScript, Swift, Kotlin or Dart.
- **Who will maintain it?** A language the bank can hire for, train in and support internally beats a better language nobody knows.
- **How long must it live?** Long-lived systems favour languages with long-term-support releases and stable ecosystems.
- **How safe is it?** Memory-safe languages remove whole classes of security bugs; strong typing catches mistakes before production.

## Legacy languages

COBOL and database procedure languages still run important parts of many banks' estates, often reliably for decades. The risk is not the language itself but the shrinking number of people who understand the code and the rules buried in it. Banks manage this by documenting business rules, wrapping legacy code behind APIs and moving logic out in stages, as described in [legacy modernisation](/architecture/modernisation/).

## Pitfalls

- **Choosing by fashion** rather than by fit and long-term support.
- **Unreviewed dependencies** pulled in from public package registries.
- **Business rules in stored procedures** that no test covers.
- **One expert** who alone understands a critical codebase.
- **Floating-point numbers for money,** instead of decimal types or integer minor units.

## Questions for a design review

- Why this language for this system, and who will maintain it in five years?
- How are third-party dependencies approved, scanned and updated?
- How are monetary amounts represented and rounded?
- Where do business rules live, and are they covered by tests?
- What happens if the one person who knows this codebase leaves?
