## Why it matters

Every other system in a bank either reads from the core or posts to it. When a payment rail credits a customer, a mobile app shows a balance or a regulator asks for a return, the answer ultimately comes from the core. Its design decides how fast a bank can launch products, how it copes with round-the-clock payments and how risky it is to change anything.

## The model explained

A core is a set of modules around one ledger. The customer module holds who the bank's customers are. The product module holds the rules: interest rates, fees, limits and statement cycles, set up as parameters rather than code, so a new savings product is usually configuration, not a software release. Accounts are instances of those products. Deposits and loans hold the balances and schedules. Every change of money ends as balanced debits and credits in the general ledger.

### Online and batch

Online processing posts each transaction as it happens and must be fast and correct. Batch processing handles work done for every account at once: accruing interest, applying charges, ageing loans, producing statements and regulatory extracts. The run that closes the business day is end of day (EOD); the run that opens the next is beginning of day (BOD).

### Why a core is hard to scale out

Money needs strong consistency: two withdrawals must never both succeed against a balance that covers only one. Most cores achieve this with a large relational database and transactions, which works well on one very big machine and less well across many. Some accounts are also hot spots. A merchant's settlement account or a bank's own clearing account may receive thousands of postings a minute, and each posting must lock the same balance. Adding servers does not help if every request queues behind one row.

### Stand-in processing and shadow balances

When the core is busy with batch work, or briefly unavailable, a stand-in layer can keep approving transactions against a shadow copy of each balance, within safe limits. Once the core is back, the stand-in replays what it approved. This keeps payments flowing but means two systems hold a version of the truth for a while, and they must be reconciled afterwards.

## Failure modes

- **End of day overruns.** Volumes grow until the batch no longer fits its window, delaying the next business day.
- **Stand-in drift.** The shadow balance and the core disagree, and a customer is allowed to overdraw or is wrongly declined.
- **Hot-account contention.** A few very busy accounts slow every posting that touches them.
- **Configuration errors.** A wrongly set product parameter charges or pays interest incorrectly across thousands of accounts at once.

## When to use it, and when to avoid it

Every bank needs a system of record, so the question is not whether to have a core but how much to put in it. Keep the core to what only it can do: accounts, balances, the ledger and product rules. Move customer journeys, payment orchestration and analytics outside it, where they can change faster. Avoid putting logic in the core simply because it is the one system that already has the data.

## Questions for a design review

- Which work must stay in the core, and which could move to a system that changes faster?
- Does end of day still fit its window at next year's expected volumes, with margin?
- What happens to payments while the core is in its batch window, and how are stand-in approvals reconciled?
- Which accounts are hot spots, and how is contention on them handled?
- How are product parameter changes reviewed and tested before they reach live accounts?
