## Why it is rarely all one or the other

Almost every bank buys its core banking platform, card systems and much of its infrastructure, and builds at least some of its customer experience and decisioning. The real questions are which capabilities belong in each group, how much to customise what is bought, and how to keep the ability to change course. This page gives a framework, not a verdict. The right answer differs by bank and changes over time.

## Patterns by type of institution

- **Public-sector banks** tend to buy large, proven platforms and run long programmes with system integrators. Scale, procurement rules and the need for stability shape those choices. <Fact id="sbi-bancs" show="claim" />
- **Large private banks** usually buy the core and build more around it: apps, decisioning and data, with sizeable in-house engineering teams.
- **Small finance banks and newer banks** often choose modern or hosted platforms to launch quickly, and build selectively where they compete.
- **Cooperative banks** frequently share a platform rather than run their own. <Fact id="ibcc-cbs" show="claim" />
- **Fintechs** build most of their product, rent infrastructure and partner with banks for the regulated parts.

These are tendencies, not rules; any single institution may differ.

## How lock-in happens

Lock-in is rarely one decision. It accumulates through customisations that only the vendor can maintain, proprietary data formats, integrations built against vendor-specific interfaces, contracts without data-export clauses, and skills that exist only at the vendor. Each of these is manageable on its own; together they can make leaving impractical. The defences are configuration over customisation, owning data in documented formats, putting your own APIs in front of bought systems, contract clauses for exit, and keeping enough in-house knowledge to run a transition.

## A worked example

This example is illustrative. A mid-sized bank wants better fraud detection for UPI payments. It rates the six factors:
- **Differentiation: high.** Fraud losses and false declines both affect customers.
- **Regulatory complexity: medium.**
- **Scale: high.**
- **Urgency: high.** Losses are rising.
- **Talent: medium.** It has data scientists but no real-time engineering team.
- **Control: high.** The models use sensitive customer data.

The factors pull in both directions, which points to a hybrid. The bank buys a proven real-time scoring engine to deploy quickly. It then builds its own features and models on its data, and keeps the right to export rules and models. It also plans, from the start, what it would take to replace the engine.

## Questions for a decision

- Would a customer notice, or care, if this capability were the same as every competitor's?
- What will it cost over ten years, including people, upgrades and exit, for both options?
- If we buy, which parts will we still build, and who owns them?
- How would we leave this vendor, how long would it take, and what would we lose?
- Which RBI outsourcing requirements does this arrangement trigger, and who will evidence them?
