## How to read this page

Each limitation is described the same way: its **cause**, its **effect**, the **common mitigation** and its **status** today. The page names an institution only where a regulator or reputable press has published the facts. Everything else describes patterns seen across the industry.

## The limitations

### 1. Cores designed for a batch era

- **Cause.** Many cores assume a nightly window when postings stop and batch work runs.
- **Effect.** Round-the-clock payments must be handled by stand-in layers during end of day, adding complexity and reconciliation.
- **Common mitigation.** Shorter, incremental batch cycles; stand-in processing; payment hubs that shield the core.
- **Status.** Widespread; eased gradually through core upgrades and modernisation.

### 2. Monolithic scaling limits

- **Cause.** One large database behind one application holds every account.
- **Effect.** Capacity is capped by the biggest machine, and busy accounts become hot spots.
- **Common mitigation.** Caching, queuing, read replicas and moving non-ledger functions out of the core.
- **Status.** Managed rather than solved at most banks.

### 3. Vendor lock-in and migration risk

- **Cause.** Heavily customised platforms, proprietary formats and long contracts.
- **Effect.** Changing vendor becomes impractical, weakening the bank's negotiating position.
- **Common mitigation.** Configuration over customisation, owned data formats, APIs in front of vendor systems and tested exit plans. <Fact id="outsourcing-concentration" show="claim" />
- **Status.** A standing risk that rises with every customisation.

### 4. Legacy skills

- **Cause.** Critical systems written in older languages or on platforms few people now learn.
- **Effect.** Knowledge concentrates in a few people; changes slow down and become riskier.
- **Common mitigation.** Documenting business rules, pairing and training, wrapping legacy code behind APIs and moving logic out in stages.
- **Status.** Growing as experienced staff retire.

### 5. Pending and reversal states

- **Cause.** Payments cross several independently run systems, and any link can time out.
- **Effect.** Customers are debited without the payee being credited, and must wait for a reversal. At about <Fact id="upi-daily-2026-08" />, even a tiny failure rate means many cases a day.
- **Common mitigation.** Unique references, automated status checks and reversal jobs, within regulatory limits. <Fact id="tat-2019" show="claim" />
- **Status.** Well governed by rules; still a common customer complaint.

### 6. Liquidity and settlement risk

- **Cause.** Customers are credited instantly while banks settle later, on a net basis.
- **Effect.** A bank carries exposure to other banks until settlement; a failure in the cycle affects many participants.
- **Common mitigation.** Settlement guarantees, limits and monitoring of intraday positions by operators and banks.
- **Status.** Managed by operators and regulators; invisible to most customers.

### 7. Retail limits versus business needs

- **Cause.** Instant rails were designed first for consumers, with per-transaction and daily ceilings.
- **Effect.** Businesses making large payments must switch rails or split payments.
- **Common mitigation.** Higher ceilings for specified merchant categories, and RTGS for large values.
- **Status.** Improving as ceilings rise for specific uses; see [payment rails](/payment-rails/).

### 8. Reconciliation burden

- **Cause.** The same transaction is recorded by the app, the switch, the payment hub, the core and the general ledger.
- **Effect.** Mismatches need investigation, often manually, and delay the resolution of customer complaints.
- **Common mitigation.** Automated matching on shared references, with exceptions routed to named owners.
- **Status.** Large operational effort at most banks.

### 9. Social-engineering fraud versus technical controls

- **Cause.** Fraudsters persuade customers to authorise payments themselves, so strong authentication is passed rather than broken.
- **Effect.** Losses continue even when systems work exactly as designed.
- **Common mitigation.** Behavioural fraud scoring, cooling-off periods for new payees, warnings at the point of payment, mule-account detection and customer education.
- **Status.** A leading and growing risk.

### 10. API security gaps between channels and the core

- **Cause.** New channels expose older functions through APIs that were never designed for direct exposure.
- **Effect.** Weaknesses such as missing authorisation checks or excessive data in responses.
- **Common mitigation.** A gateway that enforces identity and limits, fine-grained authorisation in each service, and API security testing.
- **Status.** Improving; a frequent audit finding.

### 11. Fragmented AML views

- **Cause.** Customer and transaction data sit in separate systems for deposits, cards, loans and payments.
- **Effect.** Suspicious patterns that span products are harder to see.
- **Common mitigation.** A single customer view feeding transaction monitoring, and entity resolution across systems.
- **Status.** A long-running programme at most banks.

### 12. Many regulatory returns and data-lineage risk

- **Cause.** Returns are produced from many source systems through many transformations.
- **Effect.** Errors are hard to trace, and the same figure can differ between returns.
- **Common mitigation.** A governed data warehouse, documented lineage and automated quality checks. See [data architecture](/architecture/data/).
- **Status.** Improving with investment; still labour-intensive.

### 13. Cloud adoption constraints

- **Cause.** Data residency, outsourcing duties and exit planning all apply to cloud use. <Fact id="data-localisation-2018" show="claim" /> <Fact id="outsourcing-cloud-scope" show="claim" />
- **Effect.** Slower adoption than in some other sectors, especially for systems of record.
- **Common mitigation.** Cloud regions in India, clear placement rules and reusable control evidence.
- **Status.** Adoption growing for channels, analytics and development.

### 14. Depth of disaster-recovery testing

- **Cause.** Full switch-overs are disruptive, so drills can be narrowed to what is safe.
- **Effect.** A DR site that has passed drills may still fail in a real disaster.
- **Common mitigation.** Regular, realistic drills that run real business at the DR site. <Fact id="itgrc-dr-drills" show="claim" />
- **Status.** Required by rule; depth varies in practice.

### 15. Talent asymmetry

- **Cause.** Engineering talent concentrates in large banks, technology firms and fintechs.
- **Effect.** Smaller banks depend more on vendors and struggle to keep in-house knowledge.
- **Common mitigation.** Shared platforms, managed services and focused hiring for the capabilities that matter most.
- **Status.** Persistent.

## Why "too complex to change" is not the end

Many of these limitations share one root: estates that grew by adding systems rather than replacing them. It is tempting to conclude that nothing can change without a risky replacement. In practice, banks improve step by step:
- wrap the core behind APIs;
- move one capability at a time to newer systems;
- automate reconciliation;
- test resilience for real;
- retire what is no longer needed.

Each step is small enough to reverse and valuable on its own. [Legacy modernisation](/architecture/modernisation/) describes the patterns.
